**Short answer:** if an older parent can make their own decisions, a move can be planned with their agreement and your help. If they can no longer decide about selling a home or managing money, someone usually needs legal authority to act for them, most often through a registered Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA). This is general information for England and Wales, not legal advice.

## Why Lasting Power of Attorney comes up when a parent moves

Moving a parent often involves selling a home, paying for a new one or care, and managing bills while the sale completes. These are decisions the parent has to make themselves, or someone else needs the legal right to make on their behalf. [GOV.UK explains](https://www.gov.uk/power-of-attorney) that a lasting power of attorney lets you appoint people to make decisions for you if you cannot, or no longer want to.

## The two types of LPA in England and Wales

- **Property and financial affairs.** Covers money and property decisions, which can include selling a home.
- **Health and welfare.** Covers decisions about care, medical treatment and where someone lives. It can only be used when the person has lost the capacity to make those decisions.

An LPA has to be made while the person still has the mental capacity to make it, and registered with the Office of the Public Guardian before it can be used. See [GOV.UK's guide to making a lasting power of attorney](https://www.gov.uk/power-of-attorney/make-lasting-power).

## If there is no LPA and capacity has been lost

In England and Wales, family cannot automatically act for a parent who has lost capacity. You may need to apply to the Court of Protection to be appointed as a deputy, which [GOV.UK describes here](https://www.gov.uk/become-deputy). It takes time and costs money, so it is worth speaking to a solicitor as soon as you notice a parent finding decisions difficult.

## Practical points for a move

- Check who holds an LPA, whether it is registered, and which type it is, before agreeing a sale or move date.
- An attorney must act in the person's best interests, so involve your parent in the choices wherever they can take part.
- Keep records of decisions and money spent on their behalf.
- If your parent lives in Scotland or Northern Ireland, different systems and terms apply.
