**Short answer:** choose a smaller home for the life you expect to lead over the next five to ten years, not just for today. Look at location, step-free access, storage, ongoing costs and room for future needs, visit more than once, and compare two or three options in the same way so the decision is calm rather than rushed.

*This is general information, not legal, financial or medical advice. For leasehold and other legal questions, ask a solicitor.*

## Start with where it is

The right location often matters more than the size of the rooms. Think about:

- How far it is from family and friends, and how easy it is for them to visit.
- The GP surgery, pharmacy and any regular hospital appointments.
- Local shops, and whether you can reach them on foot or by a bus you would be happy to use.
- Public transport, or parking if you plan to keep driving.
- Clubs, places of worship, and the community you want to stay part of.

If you are thinking of moving nearer your family, our guide to [later-life relocation closer to family](/blog/later-life-relocation-closer-to-family-uk/) covers the practical side.

## Step-free access and layout

Think about how you will move around the home each day, and how that could change.

- **Bungalow:** single-level living, often with a garden. See our guide to [downsizing to a bungalow](/blog/downsizing-to-a-bungalow-uk/).
- **Ground-floor flat:** no stairs, and usually less outside space to maintain.
- **Upper-floor flat:** check there is a lift, and ask how reliable it is and who is responsible for repairs.
- **Houses with stairs:** consider whether a bedroom and bathroom are available on the ground floor.

Look at the details too: the step to the front door, the width of doorways, space to turn in the hall and kitchen, and whether the bathroom has a shower you could use comfortably. [Age UK's housing options information](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/information-advice/care/housing-options/) is a helpful overview of the different types of home to consider.

## Space and storage

Rooms can feel bigger or smaller than their listing suggests, so measure them yourself or use the floor plan. Check where the bed, a favourite chair and a dining table would go, and where you would keep everyday items. Look for built-in cupboards, a loft or a garage. Our guide to [planning furniture and a floor plan for a smaller home](/blog/planning-furniture-and-floor-plan-for-a-smaller-home/) shows how to do this before you decide, and the [downsizing checklist](/blog/downsizing-checklist-what-to-keep-uk/) helps you work out what will come with you.

## Garden and upkeep

A garden can be a joy or a chore. Ask yourself honestly how much you want to look after, and whether a patio, a courtyard or shared grounds might suit you better. In a flat or retirement community, find out who maintains the communal areas and how that is paid for.

## Ongoing costs: what to ask

Do not judge a home by the asking price alone. Ask for the actual figures for the property you are viewing, and check them rather than relying on guesses.

- **Leasehold flats:** the length of the lease remaining, the service charge and what it covers, the ground rent, and how and when these can change. The [GOV.UK overview of leasehold property](https://www.gov.uk/leasehold-property) explains the basics. A solicitor can explain the specific lease you are considering.
- **Council tax:** ask which band the property is in.
- **Heating and energy:** ask for the energy performance certificate (EPC), and about the heating system and insulation.
- **Other charges:** buildings insurance, management fees, and any fees payable when you sell or pass the home on, particularly in retirement housing.

If you are considering retirement housing, our [retirement village and retirement housing guide](/blog/moving-into-a-retirement-village-uk-guide/) lists extra questions to ask about charges.

## Parking, visitors and neighbours

Check where visitors and carers can park, how far the nearest space is, and whether there is a visitor policy in shared buildings. Visit at different times, such as a weekday morning and an evening, to get a feel for noise, traffic and neighbours.

## Plan for future needs

You may not need everything now, but it is worth thinking ahead:

- Could the home be adapted later, for example with a handrail, a level-access shower or a stairlift?
- Is there a spare room or sofa bed for family to stay overnight, or for a carer if you need one?
- Is there room for mobility equipment, such as a walker or a scooter, to be stored and used indoors?
- If your needs change, how easy would it be to get support at home?

Our guide to [when to downsize](/blog/when-to-downsize-uk/) can help you think about timing, and keeping your options open is wise. Avoid committing to a home that only works for a very particular set of circumstances.

## A viewing checklist

Take this list with you, and a tape measure, and consider asking someone you trust to come along.

1. How do I get in, and are there any steps, slopes or heavy doors?
2. Where would my bed, chair and dining table go?
3. Is there enough storage for everyday items?
4. How wide are the doorways and hallways?
5. Could I use the bathroom comfortably if my mobility changed?
6. How reliable is the lift, if there is one, and who maintains it?
7. What are the service charge, ground rent and lease length, if it is leasehold?
8. What is the council tax band, and what is the EPC rating?
9. Where would visitors and carers park?
10. What are the neighbours and the street like at different times of day?
11. How long would it take me to reach the GP, shops and family?
12. Is there anything about the home I would find hard to live with in five years?
