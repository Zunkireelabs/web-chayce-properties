Short answer: pick a calm moment, ask open questions, listen for the worry behind any resistance, and treat the decision as theirs. If they say no, do not push. Explore smaller steps and come back to it. Most parents need several conversations, not one.

## Start earlier than feels comfortable

The easiest time to talk about moving is when nothing is wrong. Waiting for a fall, a hospital stay or a bereavement means decisions get made under pressure. A relaxed early conversation, even if it goes nowhere, makes later ones easier.

## How to bring it up

- **Choose the setting.** A quiet moment at their home, not in front of the whole family, and not as part of a disagreement.
- **Start with them, not the plan.** Ask what they enjoy about the house, and what has become harder: stairs, the garden, the cost of heating, being far from family.
- **Ask, don't tell.** "What would make life easier here?" opens more doors than "you can't stay here forever".
- **Share your own worry honestly.** "I worry when you are on your own on the stairs" is easier to hear than a list of reasons they should go.
- **Involve siblings early.** Parents can feel outnumbered if the conversation arrives as a united front.
- **Keep it short.** Several 20-minute conversations tend to land better than one long one.

## What if they refuse to move?

Resistance usually has a reason behind it. Common ones are:

- **Losing independence.** Moving can feel like the first step towards losing control.
- **Memories.** The home holds a lifetime, and sometimes a spouse who has died.
- **The sheer work.** Sorting decades of belongings feels impossible.
- **Cost and uncertainty.** Worry about fees, stamp duty and what to do if the new place is wrong.

If your parent has the mental capacity to decide, the choice is theirs, even if you disagree with it. Try smaller steps instead of an all-or-nothing answer:

1. Look at **adaptations** to the current home, such as a stairlift, grab rails or a downstairs bedroom.
2. Visit a **retirement community or bungalow** with no obligation, as a day out rather than a decision.
3. Agree to **revisit the conversation** in a few months.
4. Ask a trusted outside voice, such as their GP, a financial adviser or a charity like [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/), to explain the options.

## If you want to convince them to move closer to you

Many families ask how to convince a parent to move closer. In practice, convincing rarely works and can backfire. What helps more:

- Be specific about what you can offer, such as regular visits, help with the garden or school-run days with grandchildren.
- Let them visit the area first, stay a weekend and meet potential neighbours.
- Talk through what they would keep and what they would miss, including friends, clubs and their GP.
- Keep the door open. A parent who feels pressured may dig in. One who feels listened to may bring it up themselves.
