## Start With a Plan, Not a Pile of Boxes

The single most useful thing to do before moving is to stop and plan before you touch anything. Most moving stress comes not from lifting boxes but from decisions made too late — booking removals the week before completion, discovering the new home has nowhere to put the dining table, or realising on moving day that the solicitor still needs a signature. Each of these problems shares the same root cause: the work was left until it became urgent rather than handled while there was still time to think clearly. A written plan with a realistic **timeline**, a **budget** and a clear scope removes almost all of that, because it turns a vague sense of dread into a sequence of manageable, dated tasks.

## Sort and Downsize: Deciding What to Keep

Once you have a plan, the real work begins: deciding what travels with you. This is the part people dread, especially after decades in the same home, and it is almost always the most emotional stage of a move. Every cupboard holds a decision, and many of them carry memories, which is exactly why rushing this stage so often leads to regret. The goal is not to get rid of as much as possible — it is to keep what matters and let go of genuine excess without pressure or guilt.

Work room by room rather than tackling the whole house at once, so progress feels real and the task never becomes overwhelming. Measure the new home's rooms and storage first, so you know what will actually fit before you agonise over an item — there is little point debating a wardrobe that was never going to go through the door. Create simple categories — keep, give to family, donate, sell, clear — and handle one at a time. ## Handle the Admin Before Moving Day

Boxes get the attention, but paperwork causes the most lasting headaches when it is overlooked. A missed address update can follow you for months after the move is done, long after the last box is unpacked. Well before the van arrives, work through the organisations that hold your address and update each one. That means banks and building societies, your GP and any hospital clinics, pension providers, utilities and broadband, your insurers, the DVLA, the electoral roll, and any subscriptions or deliveries. Setting up mail redirection with Royal Mail gives you a safety net for anything you miss, catching the stray letters you inevitably forget.

A methodical [change-of-address checklist](https://chayceproperties.com/blog/change-of-address-checklist-uk/) stops this becoming a scramble. Alongside it, keep the move's practical coordination moving: confirm dates with estate agents and solicitors, book the removal, and agree who is doing what on the day. This coordination between estate agents, solicitors and removals is one of the things families most often underestimate — and it is precisely where having one person managing the whole move pays off. With Chayce, the same coordinator handles those conversations so you are never chasing three different parties yourself, which matters most in the final fortnight when several deadlines tend to land at once.

## The Trouble With Piecing a Move Together Yourself

You can assemble a move from separate suppliers, and plenty of people do. A volume remover such as AnyVan can shift boxes between postcodes efficiently and cheaply, a national firm like Britannia brings scale, and Age UK offers valuable general guidance for older people. Specialists such as The Senior Move Partnership focus on later-life moves too, so there is no shortage of individual options to choose from.

The catch is that most of these options still leave *you* — or a busy family member — stitching the pieces together. One company packs, another drives, someone else sorts, and the admin falls to you. Each supplier knows only its own part of the job, so the responsibility for making them all line up on the right day sits with whoever is arranging the move. For an older person, or an adult child managing it from a distance, that assembly work can quietly become the hardest part of the whole process.
